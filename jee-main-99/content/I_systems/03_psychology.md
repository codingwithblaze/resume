# Mindset, Focus & Wellbeing {#psychology}

Preparation is a long effort, and the problems in this chapter are normal parts of it. Each section names a problem, gives the evidence where there is good evidence, and a concrete routine. No quotes, no "just believe in yourself". Methods only.

## Procrastination

:::strategy Implementation intentions
Instead of "I will study Physics today", decide **when, where and what**: "At 6:30 pm, at my desk, I will open Module P11 and solve Practice Q1–Q5." A meta-analysis of 94 studies found that these if–then plans substantially increase follow-through [LS-GOLL].
:::

:::grid2
**Start small**

- **The 5-question start:** commit to five questions, not "Rotation". Starting is the hardest part, and most people continue once started.
- **Make the first step obvious:** leave the book open at the right page the night before.
- **Time blocks of 50–90 minutes** with a written target for each block.
+++
**Remove friction**

- Plan tomorrow in 5 minutes tonight: three blocks, each with a specific output.
- Keep a "parking list" on your desk: any distracting thought ("check the result date") goes on paper, to be handled at the next break.
- Track *completed blocks* in the daily tracker, not hours "sat at the desk".
:::

## Phone and screen use

:::warning The mere presence of a phone costs attention
In experiments, participants with their smartphone on the desk (even face-down and silent) performed worse on working-memory and fluid-intelligence tasks than those whose phone was in another room [LS-WARD].
:::

:::flow
Rule 1 · Another room | During study blocks the phone is outside the room, not in a bag on the desk.
Rule 2 · Scheduled checks | Check messages only in breaks (e.g. 10 minutes after every second block).
Rule 3 · Study apps on another device | If you need videos or PDFs, use a laptop or tablet with notifications off and only the needed app open.
Rule 4 · Screen curfew | No recreational screens in the hour before sleep. Screens and late messaging push bedtime later.
Rule 5 · Weekly allowance | Decide your leisure screen time for the week in advance (e.g. 1 h/day plus a longer slot on the rest day). Planned leisure is fine; unplanned scrolling is the problem.
:::

## Fear of Mathematics

:::strategy Make the fear smaller and the evidence larger
1. **Expressive writing before tests.** Writing for about 10 minutes about your worries just before an exam improved the scores of anxious students in controlled studies [LS-RAM]. Try it before every mock for three weeks.
2. **Graded exposure.** Start each Maths session with 5 questions you can certainly solve (the Must-do models, page [[maths-strategy]]), then move to harder ones. Success first builds momentum.
3. **Separate "I can't" from "I haven't yet".** Log every Maths error by type. Most turn out to be CA or MR (page [[mistake-book]]), which are habits, not ability.
4. **Timed confidence sets.** Once a week, take a 10-question set you are likely to finish. Record your time, and watch it fall over the weeks.
:::

## Mock-test anxiety

:::grid2
**Reframe the arousal**

A racing heart before a test is the body preparing to perform. Students told that arousal can *help* performance scored higher on a standardised maths test than a control group [LS-JAM]. Say it plainly: "This is energy for the paper."
+++
**Reframe the mock**

A mock is **data, not a verdict**. Its purpose is to find mistakes before the exam does. A low mock score with a good analysis is more valuable than a high one you don't learn from. Judge the average of your last three mocks, never a single one.
:::

:::tip The 60-second reset (in any mock or in the exam)
Put the pen down. Breathe in slowly for 4 counts and out for 6 counts, five times. Read the next question's last line only. Then continue with the easiest unattempted question in the section.
:::

## Burnout and energy

:::important Warning signs {{tag:rec}}
Two weeks or more of: constant tiredness despite sleep, dread of opening books, falling mock scores despite more hours, irritability, or loss of interest in things you normally enjoy. These call for a **reset week**, not more hours.
:::

:::grid3
**Sleep**

For 13–18-year-olds, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends **8–10 hours** a night [LS-AASM]. Sleep also consolidates what you learned during the day [LS-RB]. Every plan in this book protects at least 7.5–8 hours.
+++
**Movement**

The WHO recommends an average of **60 minutes a day** of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for adolescents [LS-WHO]. A 20–30-minute walk, cycle or workout still counts. Timetables include it.
+++
**Rest**

One lighter half-day per week, planned in advance. Rest is part of training. Performance falls when there is none.
:::

:::strategy The reset week
Keep the daily routine, but cut study to about 70% of normal: revision and easy questions only, no new chapters, no mocks. Sleep fully, exercise daily, and talk to someone you trust. Then resume the plan.
:::

## Motivation that lasts

:::flow
Process goals | Aim for what you control ("4 blocks today, 30 questions, Mistake Book updated"), not only outcomes ("99 percentile").
Visible progress | Use the trackers: chapters done, questions solved, mock trend. Evidence of progress sustains effort better than slogans.
A weekly win | Each Sunday, write one thing that improved this week (a faster time, a fixed mistake type).
Your reasons | Write down why this matters to you (a college, a branch, a kind of work) and keep it inside the cover.
:::

## When to talk to someone

:::warning Get help early
If low mood, anxiety, sleep problems or hopeless thoughts last for more than two weeks, or if you ever feel unsafe, talk to a parent, teacher or doctor **now**. You don't have to wait until after the exam. In India, **Tele-MANAS** (the government's free mental-health helpline) is available on **14416** or **1-800-891-4416** [TELEMANAS]. Asking for help is part of looking after your preparation, not a sign of weakness.
:::
