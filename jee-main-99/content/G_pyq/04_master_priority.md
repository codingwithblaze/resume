## The 20 most important NCERT facts

| # | Fact |
|---|---|
| 1 | $\ce{N2}$ is unreactive because of its very strong triple bond (bond enthalpy about 941 kJ/mol). |
| 2 | Solid $\ce{PCl5}$ exists as $\ce{[PCl4]+[PCl6]-}$. |
| 3 | $\ce{H3PO3}$ is dibasic (two P–OH); $\ce{H3PO2}$ is monobasic and a strong reducing agent. |
| 4 | Boric acid is a weak monobasic **Lewis** acid: it accepts $\ce{OH-}$ rather than donating $\ce{H+}$. |
| 5 | Diborane has two three-centre two-electron (B–H–B) "banana" bonds. |
| 6 | $\ce{XeF2}$ is linear, $\ce{XeF4}$ square planar, $\ce{XeF6}$ distorted octahedral; $\ce{ClF3}$ is T-shaped. |
| 7 | $\ce{O2}$ is paramagnetic; $\ce{O3}$ is diamagnetic. |
| 8 | Hydride acidity increases down group 16: $\ce{H2O} < \ce{H2S} < \ce{H2Se} < \ce{H2Te}$. |
| 9 | Mn shows the highest oxidation state (+7) in the 3d series. |
| 10 | $\ce{Cu+}$ disproportionates in water: $\ce{2Cu+ -> Cu^2+ + Cu}$. |
| 11 | The colour of $\ce{KMnO4}$ is due to charge transfer, not d–d transitions (Mn(VII) is $d^0$). |
| 12 | Lanthanoid contraction makes Zr and Hf almost the same size. |
| 13 | $\ce{Ce^4+}$ is an oxidising agent; $\ce{Eu^2+}$ and $\ce{Yb^2+}$ are reducing agents. |
| 14 | $\ce{[Ni(CO)4]}$: tetrahedral, diamagnetic · $\ce{[Ni(CN)4]^2-}$: square planar, diamagnetic · $\ce{[NiCl4]^2-}$: tetrahedral, paramagnetic. |
| 15 | EDTA is a hexadentate ligand. |
| 16 | Glucose pentaacetate doesn't react with hydroxylamine, one evidence for the cyclic structure of glucose. |
| 17 | Sucrose is non-reducing; maltose and lactose are reducing sugars. |
| 18 | Water-soluble vitamins (B, C) must be taken regularly, except $\ce{B12}$; fat-soluble ones (A, D, E, K) are stored in the body. |
| 19 | Denaturation destroys the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein; the primary structure stays intact. |
| 20 | Aniline doesn't undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions (it forms a salt with $\ce{AlCl3}$). |

## The 15 most costly mistakes

:::trap Ranked by estimated cost and frequency {{tag:rec}}
1. Misreading "incorrect", "not", "false" or "except" in the question.
2. Unit slips: cm vs m, kJ vs J, mL vs L, °C vs K.
3. Sign-convention errors (optics, thermodynamics, work in chemistry vs physics).
4. Answering a different quantity than the one asked (radius instead of diameter, $d$ instead of $d^2$).
5. Arithmetic or transcription slips on the rough sheet.
6. Using a formula outside its conditions (constant acceleration, small $\alpha$, $\lvert r\rvert < 1$).
7. Wrong $n$-factor, or $\Delta n$ counted with non-gaseous species.
8. Principal-value errors in inverse trigonometry.
9. Counting errors: the $+1$ in the number of terms, or overlapping cases in P&C.
10. Assuming events are independent, or mutually exclusive, without justification.
11. Forgetting the $\tfrac12$ (triangle area, kinetic energy, dipole energy density).
12. Over-attempting: guessing with fewer than two options eliminated.
13. Spending more than 3 minutes on one question in Round 1.
14. Not converting Section B answers as instructed (rounding, units).
15. Changing a correct answer without finding a specific error.

The Mistake Book (page [[mistake-book]]) turns this list into a prevention system.
