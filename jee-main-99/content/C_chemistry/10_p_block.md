## Cross-group principles

- **Inert pair effect:** down Groups 13–15, the $ns^2$ pair becomes reluctant to take part in bonding, so the **lower** oxidation state becomes more stable: $\ce{Tl+}$ > $\ce{Tl^{3+}}$, $\ce{Pb^{2+}}$ > $\ce{Pb^{4+}}$, $\ce{Bi^{3+}}$ > $\ce{Bi^{5+}}$. So $\ce{Pb^{4+}}$ and $\ce{Bi^{5+}}$ are strong oxidising agents, while $\ce{Sn^{2+}}$ is a reducing agent.
- **First element anomalies** (B, C, N, O, F): small size, high electronegativity, **no d orbitals** (maximum covalency 4), and the ability to form $p\pi$–$p\pi$ multiple bonds.
- **Hydrides:** thermal stability **decreases** down a group. Reducing character **increases**. H-bonding makes $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$ and HF boil anomalously high.

## Group-by-group trend cards

:::grid2
**Group 13 · B Al Ga In Tl · $ns^2np^1$**

- Oxidation states +3 and +1 (+1 more stable for Tl).
- B is a non-metal (a metalloid); the rest are metals.
- Radius: **Ga < Al** (poor 3d shielding). IE: B > Tl > Ga > Al > In.
- B is **electron-deficient**: $\ce{BX3}$ are Lewis acids. Acidity $\ce{BF3} < \ce{BCl3} < \ce{BBr3} < \ce{BI3}$ because $p\pi$–$p\pi$ back-bonding is strongest in $\ce{BF3}$.
- **Diborane** $\ce{B2H6}$: 4 terminal B–H bonds + **2 three-centre two-electron** (banana) B–H–B bridges.
- **Boric acid** $\ce{B(OH)3}$ is a weak **monobasic Lewis acid**: $\ce{B(OH)3 + H2O -> [B(OH)4]- + H+}$.
- $\ce{AlCl3}$ dimerises to $\ce{Al2Cl6}$. $\ce{Al2O3}$ is amphoteric.
- B anomaly: max covalency 4 (e.g. $\ce{[BF4]-}$), vs $\ce{[AlF6]^{3-}}$ for Al.
+++
**Group 14 · C Si Ge Sn Pb · $ns^2np^2$**

- Oxidation states +4 and +2 (+2 more stable for Pb). $\ce{SnCl2}$ is reducing; $\ce{PbO2}$ is oxidising.
- **Catenation:** C ≫ Si > Ge ≈ Sn (C–C bond strength). Pb shows virtually none.
- C anomaly: multiple bonds ($\ce{CO2}$ is molecular while $\ce{SiO2}$ is a network), max covalency 4, allotropes.
- **Allotropes of C:** diamond (sp³, hard, insulator), graphite (sp², layered, conducts), fullerene $\ce{C60}$ (sp², 20 six- and 12 five-membered rings).
- CO is neutral and toxic (binds haemoglobin); $\ce{CO2}$ is acidic. $\ce{SiO2}$ is a 3D network.
- $\ce{CCl4}$ is not hydrolysed (no d orbitals on C), while $\ce{SiCl4}$ hydrolyses readily.
- Silicones $\ce{(R2SiO)_n}$ are water-repellent. Silicates are built from $\ce{SiO4^{4-}}$ tetrahedra.
:::

:::grid2
**Group 15 · N P As Sb Bi · $ns^2np^3$**

- Oxidation states −3 to +5. +5 stability falls down the group (Bi⁵⁺ is a strong oxidant).
- **Hydrides $\ce{EH3}$:** basicity $\ce{NH3} > \ce{PH3} > \ce{AsH3} > \ce{SbH3} > \ce{BiH3}$; thermal stability falls; reducing power rises. Bond angle $\ce{NH3}$ 107.8° > $\ce{PH3}$ 93.6° > … > $\ce{BiH3}$ ~90°.
- Boiling points: $\ce{BiH3} > \ce{SbH3} > \ce{NH3} > \ce{AsH3} > \ce{PH3}$ ($\ce{NH3}$ is raised by H-bonding).
- N anomaly: $\ce{N#N}$ triple bond (very inert $\ce{N2}$); no $\ce{NCl5}$ (no d orbitals), whereas $\ce{PCl5}$ exists.
- Oxides of N: $\ce{N2O}$, NO neutral; $\ce{N2O3}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{N2O5}$ acidic. $\ce{NO2}$ is brown and paramagnetic, and dimerises to $\ce{N2O4}$.
- Oxoacids of P: $\ce{H3PO2}$ (monobasic, 2 P–H, strong reducer), $\ce{H3PO3}$ (dibasic, 1 P–H, reducer), $\ce{H3PO4}$ (tribasic). **Basicity = number of P–OH groups.**
- White P ($\ce{P4}$, reactive, glows) vs red P (polymeric, stable). $\ce{PCl5}$ is trigonal bipyramidal; in the solid it is $\ce{[PCl4]+[PCl6]-}$.
+++
**Group 16 · O S Se Te Po · $ns^2np^4$**

- O shows −2 (−1 in peroxides, +2 in $\ce{OF2}$). S, Se, Te: +2, +4, +6.
- **Hydrides $\ce{H2E}$:** acidity $\ce{H2O} < \ce{H2S} < \ce{H2Se} < \ce{H2Te}$; thermal stability falls; boiling point $\ce{H2O}$ ≫ $\ce{H2Te} > \ce{H2Se} > \ce{H2S}$.
- O anomaly: H-bonding, $\ce{O2}$ paramagnetic, max covalency 4. $\ce{SF6}$ exists but "$\ce{OF6}$" doesn't.
- **Ozone** $\ce{O3}$: bent, diamagnetic, strong oxidant; formed by silent electric discharge.
- **Sulphur allotropes:** rhombic (α, stable below 369 K) and monoclinic (β); $\ce{S8}$ puckered crown ring.
- $\ce{SF6}$ is exceptionally inert (steric protection). $\ce{H2SO4}$ is dibasic, dehydrating and oxidising.
:::

:::grid2
**Group 17 · F Cl Br I At · $ns^2np^5$**

- F shows only −1. The others show −1, +1, +3, +5, +7.
- Oxidising power: $\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}$.
- **Bond dissociation enthalpy:** $\ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{F2} > \ce{I2}$. F–F is anomalously weak (lone-pair repulsion).
- **Acidity of HX (aq):** HF < HCl < HBr < HI. HF has the highest boiling point (H-bonding).
- Colours: $\ce{F2}$ pale yellow, $\ce{Cl2}$ greenish yellow, $\ce{Br2}$ red-brown, $\ce{I2}$ violet (vapour).
- Oxoacid strength: HOCl < $\ce{HClO2}$ < $\ce{HClO3}$ < $\ce{HClO4}$. Oxidising power runs the other way.
- **Interhalogens:** XX′ (linear), XX′₃ (T-shaped, e.g. $\ce{ClF3}$), XX′₅ (square pyramidal, $\ce{BrF5}$), XX′₇ (pentagonal bipyramidal, $\ce{IF7}$). They are more reactive than the halogens (except $\ce{F2}$).
- F anomaly: most electronegative element, no d orbitals, only one oxoacid (HOF).
+++
**Group 18 · He Ne Ar Kr Xe Rn · $ns^2np^6$**

- Very high IE (decreasing down the group); positive electron gain enthalpy; monatomic gases.
- **First noble-gas compound:** $\ce{Xe+[PtF6]-}$ (Bartlett, 1962). The idea came from $\ce{O2+[PtF6]-}$, because the IE of $\ce{O2}$ ≈ IE of Xe.
- Xenon fluorides: $\ce{XeF2}$ linear, $\ce{XeF4}$ square planar, $\ce{XeF6}$ distorted octahedral. Oxides: $\ce{XeO3}$ pyramidal, $\ce{XeOF4}$ square pyramidal.
- Hydrolysis of $\ce{XeF6}$ gives $\ce{XeO3}$ (complete).
- Uses: He (balloons, diving gas mixtures, low-temperature work), Ar (inert atmosphere, bulbs), Ne (signs).
- He has the lowest boiling point of any substance.
:::

## Unique behaviour of the first element: summary

| Group | First element | Key anomalies |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | B | non-metal; covalent compounds; max covalency 4; electron-deficient hydrides (diborane) |
| 14 | C | catenation; $p\pi$–$p\pi$ bonds; allotropes; max covalency 4 |
| 15 | N | diatomic gas with a triple bond; no pentahalides; H-bonding in $\ce{NH3}$ |
| 16 | O | diatomic paramagnetic gas; H-bonding; mainly −2 state |
| 17 | F | only −1; weak F–F bond; HF weak acid with H-bonds; strongest oxidant |

## Common traps

:::trap Mistake alerts
- "Stronger Lewis acid = more electronegative halogen" is **wrong** for $\ce{BX3}$. Back-bonding reverses the order.
- Basicity of oxoacids of P = number of **P–OH**, not the total number of H atoms.
- Bond energy of $\ce{F2}$ is **lower** than $\ce{Cl2}$ and $\ce{Br2}$.
- Acidity of hydrides increases down Groups 16 and 17 (bond strength decreases), even though electronegativity decreases.
:::

## Question patterns

:::pyq Recurring structures
1. Ordering properties (acidity, basicity, bond energy, boiling point, stability) of hydrides/halides/oxoacids down a group.
2. Inert-pair-effect consequences (oxidising/reducing behaviour).
3. Shapes and bonding of landmark compounds ($\ce{B2H6}$, $\ce{PCl5}$, xenon fluorides, interhalogens).
4. Basicity of phosphorus oxoacids.
5. Anomalous behaviour of the first element.
:::
